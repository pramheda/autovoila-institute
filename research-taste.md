# Research Taste

This is the institute's working guide to choosing consequential research.

It is adapted in spirit from the research philosophy used by
[AutoVoila](https://github.com/paraschopra/autovoila), especially its emphasis
on surprisingness, downstream fruitfulness, alternative explanations, and
feasibility. The exact upstream file is not redistributed here because the
repository does not currently display an explicit licence.

This document extends those ideas for an AI-native institute whose canonical
output is a navigable research world rather than a paper.

## 1. Research begins with taste

There are infinitely many measurable questions.

The difficult step is identifying which questions, if answered, would change
how capable researchers understand or act in the world.

Spend substantial reasoning effort before committing large compute or human
attention.

A strong question is not merely novel. It is worth knowing.

## 2. Four primary tests

### Surprising to informed experts

Ask:

- What would a strong domain expert predict before seeing the result?
- Is the proposed claim already known under another name?
- Is it merely new to this institute?
- What result would genuinely update an expert?

Novelty requires serious literature work and contact with the actual frontier.

Do not mistake lack of retrieval for a research gap.

### Fruitful downstream

Ask:

- If this is true, what changes?
- Which new experiments, systems, or research programmes become possible?
- Does the result alter a useful representation?
- Does it change a decision or only add a fact?
- Would the finding remain interesting after the benchmark or model version changes?

Prefer work that opens a productive landscape over small improvements on
isolated measures that few people care about.

### Forecloses alternative explanations

A good result should distinguish among live explanations.

Before an experiment, state:

- the primary claim;
- the strongest alternatives;
- the prediction of each;
- the confounds;
- the controls;
- and what outcome would weaken the preferred view.

Use replications, baselines, ablations, negative controls, capability controls,
blind evaluation, and independent analysis where they actually bear on the
claim.

Do not make claims broader than the sampled tasks, models, data, or training
pathways justify.

### Feasible with available reality

Ambition must meet:

- time;
- compute;
- data;
- tool access;
- model access;
- engineering ability;
- statistical power;
- and human attention.

A smaller discriminating experiment is often more valuable than a grand
programme that cannot be completed or interpreted.

Feasibility does not mean choosing trivial questions. It means choosing the
smallest tractable system that preserves the important scientific structure.

## 3. The ambition–evidence frontier

Narrow claims are easier to support but may be inconsequential.

Broad claims may matter more but require much more evidence and stronger
exclusion of alternatives.

The institute should explicitly choose where it is operating on this frontier.

Do not use broad language to market narrow evidence.

Do not shrink an important question into a benchmark improvement merely
because that is easy to score.

## 4. Search over representations

Question selection includes choosing the unit of analysis.

Depending on the charter, possible units may include:

- actor;
- process;
- mechanism;
- intervention;
- environment;
- measurement;
- artifact;
- decision;
- constraint;
- or null result.

A better representation may produce more scientific progress than a better
experiment under the old representation.

At major representation choices, invoke the polymath and an independent
skeptic.

## 5. Phases of research

These phases are guides, not a rigid pipeline.

### Exploration

Time-box a broad search for promising, surprising, and discriminating effects.

Use cheap spikes, toy systems, literature reconnaissance, and alternative
framings.

Preserve several branches when useful.

Label all results exploratory.

### Question sharpening

Turn a promising effect into a precise claim.

Ask:

- Why should anyone care?
- What exactly is new?
- What are the strongest alternatives?
- What evidence would license the intended scope?
- Can the work be completed credibly?

### Experiment execution

Run experiments designed around the actual claim.

Do not let implementation convenience silently redefine the question.

Preserve failures, nulls, deviations, code, data, prompts, and raw outputs.

### Confirmation

When a specific claim matters enough, freeze the protocol and test it using
fresh or reserved evidence.

Do not adapt the test until it becomes positive.

### Communication

Create papers, reports, visualisations, or websites only as views over the
research world.

Communication must not become the optimisation target of the research.

## 6. Simplified tests versus real-world relevance

A strong synthetic condition can establish that a mechanism is possible.

It does not establish prevalence or importance in realistic data.

Always distinguish:

1. possibility;
2. mechanism;
3. prevalence;
4. effect size;
5. and scientific significance.

## 7. Research taste under uncertainty

Some choices remain underdetermined by current metrics:

- which question is important;
- which ontology is illuminating;
- which anomaly deserves pursuit;
- which null result changes expectations;
- and when a field is ready for a broader claim.

Agents should prepare these decisions by presenting evidence, alternatives,
costs, and predictions.

The human may decide them.

## 8. Warning signs

Pause and reassess when work is dominated by:

- polished prose without decisive evidence;
- several correlated agents agreeing;
- analogy without changed experiments;
- infrastructure without a current bottleneck;
- evaluator optimisation without evaluator validation;
- a claim that expands as results weaken;
- branches being closed to simplify the story;
- or a paper deadline becoming the reason the research continues.

## 9. Research-selection note

Before major resource allocation, write a brief free-form note explaining:

- the question;
- why it is surprising;
- why it is fruitful;
- the strongest alternatives;
- the proposed discriminating evidence;
- feasibility;
- and the cheapest result that would cause the institute to stop or pivot.

The note is a thinking aid, not a bureaucratic form.
