# Adaptive Hiring of Intelligence

## 1. Purpose

This document describes how the institute decides what additional cognition to instantiate.

It is not a permanent organisation chart.

It is a living meta-policy.

The institute should revise its operational beliefs as:

- models improve;
- agent methods change;
- new research appears;
- tools change;
- and the institute accumulates local evidence.

## 2. Stable principle

Start with the smallest sufficient organisation.

Hire another agent only when it is expected to provide one or more of:

- additional parallel search;
- a genuinely different model trajectory;
- context isolation;
- specialised competence;
- a different tool or dataset;
- adversarial pressure;
- clean-room replication;
- independent selection;
- or a representation-changing perspective.

The relevant quantity is not agent count.

It is the number and quality of complementary information channels.

## 3. Questions before hiring

Before spawning substantial work, ask:

1. Is the task primarily sequential or parallel?
2. Is there a reliable objective evaluator?
3. Does one agent need continuous ownership of the context?
4. Is correlated error a serious risk?
5. Would isolated context reduce anchoring?
6. Is a different model family likely to add a different inductive bias?
7. Does the task need specialised tools or knowledge?
8. Is the bottleneck idea generation, verification, execution, or selection?
9. What will combine the outputs?
10. How will weak outputs be rejected?
11. What is the coordination cost?
12. What is the stopping rule?
13. Would human judgment add more value than another agent?

## 4. Provisional current heuristics

These are hypotheses, not constitutional truths.

They should be reviewed as models and evidence change.

### Sequential coding with tests

Prefer:

- one coherent coding owner;
- optional independent verifier;
- additional attempts only after clear failure or architectural ambiguity.

Avoid:

- large debates;
- many agents editing the same files;
- and consensus over code that can be directly tested.

### Objective best-of-many search

When a reliable evaluator exists, parallel sampling may be valuable.

Examples:

- passing tests;
- benchmark score;
- proof verification;
- rendering validity;
- prediction error;
- or simulation performance.

Use several independent attempts and select by evidence.

### Open-ended question formation

Use:

- one owner;
- a small number of isolated alternative framers;
- and possibly one polymath.

Do not begin with dozens of homogeneous agents.

Inspect whether the first wave produced genuinely different regions of the question space before expanding.

### High-stakes interpretation without an objective verifier

Use:

- one domain owner;
- one independent skeptic;
- a different model family when available;
- clean evidence presentation;
- and human judgment.

Avoid averaging all views.

### Replication

Use an independent branch with limited access to the expected result.

Prefer a different implementation path when possible.

### Architecture and ontology choices

Use:

- one owner;
- a requirements-only polymath;
- one critic;
- and human selection if the choice is consequential or hard to reverse.

### Routine work

Use one capable, cost-effective agent.

Do not invoke special organisations for ordinary retrieval, formatting, or known execution.

## 5. Polymath hiring

Hire a polymath when the likely value comes from a changed representation, not from more domain execution.

A polymath is most useful:

- before a major commitment;
- when the team is trapped in native vocabulary;
- when an anomaly resists standard explanations;
- when architecture has accumulated complexity;
- or when broader mechanisms may connect fields.

A polymath is less useful for:

- debugging a known error;
- running a routine evaluation;
- copying data;
- or applying an established method.

## 6. Same model versus different model

Another call to the same model can provide:

- more sampling;
- a different trajectory;
- or context isolation.

It does not guarantee independent judgment.

A different model family may reduce correlated failure, but it may also be weaker, more expensive, or less tool-capable.

Choose model diversity when:

- correlated error is costly;
- the task is not objectively verifiable;
- the decision is consequential;
- or the model families have materially different training or post-training.

Do not use model diversity as a ritual.

## 7. Prompt diversity

Prompts can route models into different learned procedures and conceptual neighbourhoods.

Weak prompt diversity:

- cosmetic rewrites;
- role labels without changed evidence or method;
- slightly different tones.

Stronger prompt diversity:

- different decompositions;
- different evidence access;
- different objectives;
- different cognitive operations;
- different failure criteria;
- and clean-room framing.

Prefer explicit operations over celebrity simulation.

## 8. Hiring memo

For nontrivial team formation, create a short free-form hiring note.

The note should normally explain:

- the task;
- why one owner is or is not enough;
- what complementary channels are needed;
- which models or tools are chosen;
- what remains isolated;
- how outputs will be verified;
- how selection will occur;
- expected cost;
- and when to stop or escalate.

Do not enforce a rigid form if another representation is better.

## 9. Learning from local experience

After a meaningful agent configuration completes, record:

- task type;
- agents and models used;
- prompts or cognitive programmes;
- information access;
- topology;
- cost and time;
- unique contributions;
- duplicated work;
- correlated errors;
- whether the output survived later scrutiny;
- whether it changed the research;
- human attention consumed;
- and what organisation would be chosen next time.

External research provides a prior.

The institute’s actual outcomes provide local evidence.

## 10. Updating the playbook

Refresh this playbook when:

- major models change;
- a major orchestration method appears;
- internal experience repeatedly contradicts current heuristics;
- a costly new organisational pattern is proposed;
- or a scheduled review is due.

A review should:

1. search recent primary research;
2. assess whether the results transfer to the institute’s tasks;
3. inspect local hiring history;
4. identify stale assumptions;
5. propose limited updates;
6. state uncertainty;
7. and preserve human review for foundational changes.

## 11. What agents may update autonomously

Agents may update provisional operational beliefs such as:

- which model performs best on a coding task;
- how many samples are useful for a specific evaluator;
- whether a polymath improved architecture work;
- or whether one verifier consistently catches leakage.

Agents should propose, rather than silently adopt, changes to:

- scientific confirmation standards;
- human interruption policy;
- preservation of dissent;
- core safety controls;
- truth-versus-appearance priorities;
- or other constitutional principles.

## 12. Adaptive escalation ladder

A useful default escalation ladder is:

### Level 0: one owner

Start with one strong coherent agent.

### Level 1: additional sampling

Use more trajectories from the same model when the task is verifiable or candidate generation is the bottleneck.

### Level 2: specialist programme

Instantiate a distinct capability such as:

- coding;
- statistics;
- literature;
- polymathic reasoning;
- or experimental design.

### Level 3: isolated adversary or replication

Use when anchoring or confirmation bias is the primary risk.

### Level 4: heterogeneous models

Use when correlated failure remains important and the decision is consequential.

### Level 5: human attention

Escalate when the remaining choice depends on taste, importance, responsibility, or an irreducible value judgment.

This ladder is a starting point, not a law.

## 13. Selection policy

The selector is often as important as the generators.

Selection should use:

- evidence;
- tests;
- explicit uncertainty;
- provenance;
- and failure analysis.

Do not reveal model prestige when it is irrelevant.

Do not synthesize before weak candidates are rejected.

When no candidate is clearly superior, preserve the disagreement.
